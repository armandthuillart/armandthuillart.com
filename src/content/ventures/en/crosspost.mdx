Crosspost was one of the projects I built while learning web development in my free time. The idea was simple: write a social post in your own voice and publish it everywhere at once.

I thought the idea would sell itself. I learned that “post everywhere” explains the feature, not why someone should care. Someone who posts once a month has a different problem from a creator who has to keep several accounts active. I had to figure out who had the problem often enough to change what they were already doing.

I didn't know how to sell software. I started out describing the product. Later I tried asking how people wrote posts, where they shared them, and what made the process annoying. It gave me a better pitch, and sometimes it showed me that the person didn't need Crosspost. That was useful too. I didn't need everyone to want it. I needed to understand who did.

Crosspost showed me that writing code and selling a product are two different jobs. I could build the tool, but I still had to find the people who wanted it and explain why it mattered. I use that lesson with Dimsight now. Selling a custom machine is a much bigger decision, but the conversation still starts by finding out what the buyer is trying to do.
